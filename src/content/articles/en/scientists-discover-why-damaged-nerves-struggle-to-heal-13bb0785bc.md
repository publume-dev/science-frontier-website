Nerve injuries often lead to limited regeneration due to intrinsic neuronal mechanisms. Investigators at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai pinpointed the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), previously known mainly as a toxin sensor, as a crucial regulator of axon regrowth.

The study showed that active AHR signaling suppresses axon growth. In mouse models of peripheral nerve damage and spinal cord injury, removing AHR from neurons or using drugs that block its activity led to greater regeneration and improved recovery of movement and sensation. Mechanistically, AHR supports proteostasis, which helps injured neurons manage stress but limits production of new proteins needed for axon regrowth; suppressing AHR shifts priorities toward growth, and this response depends on HIF-1α.

Several AHR-inhibiting drugs are already in clinical trials for other conditions, raising the possibility of testing them for nerve injuries—though the work remains early, with further research needed on timing, dosage, and effects on other cells. The report did not provide quantitative effect sizes, statistical significance, or specific methods for assessing regeneration and functional recovery.
