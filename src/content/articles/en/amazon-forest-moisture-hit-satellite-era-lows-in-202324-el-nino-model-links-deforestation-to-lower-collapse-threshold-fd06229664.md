During the 2023–24 El Niño, droughts pushed radar measurements of forest moisture and living plant matter in the Amazon to their lowest levels since satellite records began in 1992. Researchers estimate that less than half of the forest affected by those droughts will recover to pre-drought conditions within seven years, the slowest recovery from a major Amazon drought in the satellite record. Wildfires ignited during the event produced the strongest carbon emissions from the Amazon since 2010, according to Luciana Gatti. How the current El Niño develops remains uncertain, with the outcome depending on its strength and on how much of the forest burns.

A model that simulated atmospheric moisture movement compared two futures. With no deforestation, the Amazon would stay relatively stable under global warming of up to 3.7–4 °C above pre-industrial levels. But if 22–28% of the rainforest is deforested, warming of 1.5–1.9 °C could trigger a collapse that turns up to 80% of the forest into degraded woodland and savannah-like ecosystems. The comparison is relevant because, according to Nico Wunderling, the Amazon is about 17% deforested depending on how it is measured, and Luciana Gatti’s research shows the southeastern Amazon has already switched from a carbon sink to a carbon source, releasing more carbon than it stores.

The exact location and proximity of the Amazon’s tipping point remain debated: some researchers think parts of the forest have already crossed local tipping points, while others say the basin-wide threshold has not been reached. The model behind the 1.5–1.9 °C estimate did not fully account for wildfires, which could increase the forest’s vulnerability and bring the tipping point closer.
