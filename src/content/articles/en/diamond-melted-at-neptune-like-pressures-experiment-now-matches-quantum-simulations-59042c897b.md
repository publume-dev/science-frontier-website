Previous experiments had measured diamond melting temperatures that disagreed with quantum mechanical computer simulations by about 20%, a discrepancy that remained unresolved for two decades. Understanding how diamond melts under extreme pressures is crucial for inertial confinement fusion research and for modeling the interiors of ice giant planets such as Neptune and Uranus, where 'diamond rain' is thought to occur.

Using laser-driven dynamic compression at the Omega Laser Facility, researchers measured diamond melting at pressures exceeding those at Earth's core by a factor of three. X-ray diffraction showed that diamond retains its diamond structure all the way up to melting, with no intermediate crystalline phase observed under single-shock compression. The new melting temperatures closely match quantum-based simulations, thereby resolving the prior discrepancy.

Applying the new melting data to inertial confinement fusion experiments could allow the use of slightly slower initial shocks, potentially tripling fusion energy gain—but this is a model-based estimate that assumes other performance-degrading processes can be controlled, and direct experimental evidence of this gain is lacking. Also, the absence of an intermediate phase is specific to single-shock compression; under multi-shock or different loading conditions, such a phase might still occur.
