The thylacine, often called the Tasmanian tiger, has a skull that has been difficult to interpret. It weighed about half as much as a gray wolf on average, yet its skull was comparable in size to a gray wolf's skull.

Researchers found that the thylacine's skull featured an oversized, long, and tall upper jaw with a widened snout—a combination that does not match any known living carnivorous mammal. They propose that the thylacine likely used its long jaws to deliver high-impact snapping bites to subdue small- to medium-sized prey such as bandicoots, rather than holding down large animals like sheep.

The study was published in Nature Communications in 2026 and involved researchers from Flinders University and the University of Melbourne, but the report does not fully describe the analytical methods or statistical significance, so the strength of the conclusions remains to be verified.
