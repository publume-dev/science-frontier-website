dcLVA is a surgical procedure that connects lymphatic vessels in the neck to veins, aiming to improve drainage of waste from the brain. It was first performed on an Alzheimer's patient in 2020.

Small studies from China suggest the surgery can reduce amyloid-beta and tau proteins in cerebrospinal fluid and improve cognitive scores, but most lacked control groups and had modest average gains. The largest cohort so far, involving over 100 patients with severe Alzheimer's, reported modest improvements in cognitive and functional scores alongside declines in amyloid-beta and tau levels after dcLVA. Imaging studies show increased connectivity in the brain's default mode network and increased blood flow through neck vessels after surgery, but this evidence is preliminary.

Experts express skepticism about the durability of benefits and the rapid symptom improvement, which is not fully explained by current mechanistic understanding. The mechanism of action for dcLVA is not fully understood, and the speed of improvement is difficult to explain. It is unclear whether observed benefits represent true disease modification or temporary effects, and anecdotal reports suggest many patients may backslide within a year. After rapid adoption without hard evidence, Chinese regulators restricted the procedure to formal clinical research settings.
