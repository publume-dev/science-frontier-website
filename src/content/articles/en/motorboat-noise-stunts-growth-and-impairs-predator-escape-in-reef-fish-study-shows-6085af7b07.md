Anthropogenic noise is an increasingly common stressor in marine environments, yet its effects on fish during early developmental stages remain poorly understood. Researchers from the University of Exeter, University of Bristol, and James Cook University conducted a controlled experiment to investigate the impact of motorboat noise on coral reef fish.

In the study published in Environmental Pollution, researchers exposed spiny chromis embryos and juveniles to motorboat noise or natural reef sounds for up to 78 days, with tests conducted in quiet conditions 24 hours after sound exposure. Results showed that fish exposed to motorboat noise were on average 7% shorter than those exposed to natural reef sounds. In predator escape trials, fish exposed to motorboat noise during both embryonic and juvenile stages were less likely to respond to a simulated predator attack (68% responded) compared to about 80% for fish exposed to reef sounds at least once; of those that responded, 40% of the boat-noise group swam toward the predator versus about 20% in the reef-sound groups.

The findings indicate that motorboat noise can affect coral reef fish by slowing growth and impairing predator escape responses, even under laboratory playback conditions. However, the extent to which these effects translate to natural reef conditions with variable boat traffic and other stressors is not directly assessed, leaving the real-world impact uncertain.
