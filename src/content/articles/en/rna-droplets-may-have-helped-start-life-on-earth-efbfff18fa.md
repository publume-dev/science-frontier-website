In the RNA world hypothesis for the origin of life, RNA can both store information and catalyze reactions. But how RNA concentrated in primordial environments to form functional structures has remained unclear. Liquid droplet formation via phase separation has been proposed as one possible mechanism.

Researchers found that RNA begins forming droplets at temperatures roughly 10 degrees Celsius lower than single-stranded DNA with essentially the same sequences. This difference stems from the 2'-hydroxyl group in RNA's sugar, which helps RNA interact more strongly with magnesium ions and retain fewer water molecules around its backbone than DNA. Chemically modifying the 2'-OH group to 2'-Ome weakened RNA's tendency to condense and altered whether the resulting condensates remained fluid or became gel-like. Additionally, RNA molecules more readily formed interconnected networks within droplets, transforming the material from fluid-like to gel-like under heat.

The study reveals a chemical advantage for RNA in droplet formation, potentially supporting the RNA world hypothesis. However, these experiments were conducted in vitro and in simulations, so implications for prebiotic Earth remain speculative.
