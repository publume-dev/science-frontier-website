Viviparity (giving birth to live young) is a defining feature of mammals, but its evolutionary origin has long been debated. Traditionally, viviparity was thought to have appeared relatively late, with the earliest direct evidence coming from eutherian fossils around 150 million years ago.

Researchers identified a neonatal line in the bone microstructure of a 236-million-year-old cynodont fossil, a key indicator of live birth. The newborn's body mass was about 14% of the adult's (1.7 kg vs. 12 kg), which is far higher than in reptiles (0.1%–0.6%) and birds (1.3%–4.5%) and within the range of extant placental mammals (up to 18.77%). This suggests viviparity may have originated in the mammalian lineage at least 95–90 million years earlier than previously believed.

The finding could rewrite the timeline of mammalian evolution, but the conclusion rests on interpreting a single growth mark as a neonatal line, which the authors acknowledge is indirect evidence. They also caution that this could be an isolated case and that more fossils are needed to confirm an earlier origin of viviparity in cynodonts.
