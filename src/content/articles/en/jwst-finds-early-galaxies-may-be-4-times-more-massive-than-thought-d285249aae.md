Researchers used data from the James Webb Space Telescope and the Very Large Telescope to examine nine massive, mature galaxies from the early universe that had stopped forming stars. Earlier studies typically assumed that stellar mass distributions are similar everywhere in the cosmos.

The team separated each galaxy's light into a spectrum, detecting subtle signatures of faint, low-mass stars hidden among brighter ones. They found that these galaxies contain a much larger proportion of low-mass stars than earlier estimates, leading to mass estimates three to four times higher than previous calculations. One galaxy, likely formed less than 1.5 billion years after the Big Bang, could be as much as four times more massive than previously thought.

The findings challenge the assumption that stellar mass distributions are similar across the universe, with potential implications for galaxy formation models and possibly the number of planets in the early universe. However, the study is based on a limited sample, and further observations are needed to confirm the broader applicability.
