The study analyzed data from 91,292 UK Biobank participants who wore activity monitors for 7 days, with a median follow-up of 12.38 years. Prolonged sedentary behavior was defined as bouts of at least 30 minutes with at least 90% of that time spent sedentary. The observational design cannot prove that sitting causes cancer, and the cohort may have a health volunteer bias.

Each additional hour of prolonged, uninterrupted sedentary behavior per day was associated with a 9% higher risk of dying from cancer (HR 1.09; 95% CI 1.06, 1.11). Substituting one hour per day of that sedentary time with light physical activity was associated with a 12% lower risk (HR 0.88; 95% CI 0.79, 0.99). Interrupted sedentary behavior was associated with lower risk across all examined outcomes, including cancer incidence and mortality.

These findings come from a single cohort with limited generalizability and no details on sedentary contexts such as work or driving, so they should be interpreted cautiously. The link is correlational, not causal, but the data suggest that reducing uninterrupted sitting or breaking it up with light activity may be associated with lower cancer mortality risk.
