Alzheimer's disease is a common form of dementia characterized by brain changes such as amyloid plaques and tangles. Hormone therapy has been used to relieve menopausal symptoms, but its effects on cognitive health have been debated. In this new study, researchers analyzed data from 21,462 women, some of whom used estrogen-only hormone therapy in later life. Participants started therapy at an average age of 70, whereas current standard practice typically begins in the late 40s to early 50s and stops before age 60.

After adjusting for age, education, genetics, race, and hypertension, women who used estrogen-only hormone therapy had 35% lower odds of showing signs of Alzheimer's disease at autopsy compared with non-users. Specifically, 18% of hormone therapy users showed no signs of Alzheimer's disease versus 10% of non-users, while 40% of users showed all three signs versus 51% of non-users. Hormone therapy use was also associated with 39% lower odds of a clinical dementia diagnosis, and users were less likely to show memory problems or declines in daily functioning. Analyses of biomarkers collected while women were alive indicated that hormone therapy users had amyloid levels consistent with less accumulation in the brain.

This observational study shows an association between estrogen-only hormone therapy and reduced Alzheimer's pathology and clinical dementia risk, but the authors note it does not prove causation and more research is needed before recommendations can be made. Additionally, because the therapy was used differently in the study (starting at an average age of 70) than in current practice (typically starting before age 60), the findings may not apply to modern hormone therapy use.
