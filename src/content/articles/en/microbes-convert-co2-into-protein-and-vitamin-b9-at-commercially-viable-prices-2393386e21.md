Global food production faces resource and environmental pressures, and using microbes to convert carbon dioxide into food ingredients is one potential solution. However, the commercial viability of this approach has previously been unclear.

A study published in PNAS conducted a techno-economic analysis of a two-stage bioreactor facility. The facility, with a volume of 1,750 cubic meters, could produce 12.9 kilotons of yeast annually, containing 813 kilograms of folic acid and 5.6 kilotons of protein. These products would be sufficient to meet the recommended daily dose of vitamin B9 for 5.6 million people and 5% of their protein needs. The analysis showed that at a price of $20 per kilogram of enriched yeast, the facility would pay for itself in five years. Under ideal conditions, if the cost of producing carbon dioxide were zero and the yeast production rate could be increased, the minimum economically viable price could drop to $4.53 per kilogram. If the yeast must be heat-treated for purification to fully supply people with protein, the minimum price rises to $14.24 per kilogram, extending the payback period to seven years at a price of $20.

The study's conclusions are based on a sample calculation and assumptions; actual industrial performance may vary. Economic viability is highly sensitive to electricity prices and raw material costs, which may fluctuate. The study reports that no technical problems were encountered in scaling up in either production model, but the article does not provide details on the validation of this scale-up or the basis for the 'no technical problems' claim.
