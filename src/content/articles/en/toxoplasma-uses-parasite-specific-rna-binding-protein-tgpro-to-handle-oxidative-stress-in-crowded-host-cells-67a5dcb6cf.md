Toxoplasma parasites reproduce inside host cells, and as the parasite population grows, the cells become crowded—a situation that brings metabolic stress, including oxidative stress. To find the genes that matter under those conditions, researchers ran a genome-wide CRISPR screen that measured parasite fitness at high population density. The screen singled out a previously uncharacterized protein they named TgPRO.

TgPRO is an RNA-binding protein that helps control genes involved in energy production and iron use. It binds and stabilizes messenger RNAs needed for nutrient use, mitochondrial activity, and iron-sulfur cluster assembly, giving parasites a way to manage oxidative stress. When TgPRO is not functional, parasites accumulate more reactive oxygen molecules and struggle to compete at high density. Lowering oxygen levels reduced that buildup of reactive oxygen and partially restored growth of parasites lacking TgPRO.

TgPRO looks like a parasite-specific invention: it shares little resemblance to metabolic regulators in mammals, yeast, and bacteria, suggesting convergent evolution rather than a shared ancestral mechanism. The pathway also appears relevant during infection—mice infected with parasites lacking functional TgPRO developed smaller brain cysts. Whether that pathway can be exploited therapeutically remains untested.
