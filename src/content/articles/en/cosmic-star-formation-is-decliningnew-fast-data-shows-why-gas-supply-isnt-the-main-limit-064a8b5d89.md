The universe is filled with hydrogen, yet the star formation rate has been declining. Over the past 4.5 billion years, the cosmic star formation rate has dropped to less than half its previous level. Using the Five hundred meter Aperture Spherical radio Telescope (FAST) and data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), researchers studied about 2.5 million galaxies. They found that neutral atomic hydrogen density 4.5 billion years ago was only about 1.4 times higher than today.

The star formation rate 4.5 billion years ago was approximately 2.5 times higher than today, but neutral hydrogen density was only 1.4 times higher. This discrepancy indicates that rapid depletion of neutral hydrogen alone cannot explain the decline. The findings suggest the key changes may involve how gas moves through the baryon cycle, such as reduced efficiency in converting neutral hydrogen to molecular gas, rather than the total amount of neutral hydrogen.

This study provides a new perspective on galaxy evolution: the slowdown in star formation may not be due to running out of fuel, but rather changes in the efficiency of gas recycling.
