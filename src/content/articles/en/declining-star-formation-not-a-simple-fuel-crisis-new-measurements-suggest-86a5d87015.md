The long-term decline in cosmic star formation is a central issue in galaxy evolution, often attributed to the exhaustion of neutral hydrogen gas—the fuel for star formation. However, this simple 'fuel crisis' scenario has lacked direct observational testing.

Using data from the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST) and the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), researchers stacked signals from approximately 2.5 million galaxies to measure the star formation rate density and neutral hydrogen (HI) density about 4.5 billion years ago (redshift ~0.4). They found that the star formation rate then was about 2.5 times higher than today, while the HI density was only about 1.4 times the current level. This disproportionate decline suggests that gas supply alone does not dictate the downturn.

The finding indicates that the falling star formation rate cannot be fully explained by a reduction in neutral hydrogen mass; other factors, such as a decrease in star formation efficiency, likely play a role. However, the report does not provide uncertainty estimates for the measured ratios, and details of the statistical analysis are not fully described, so the results should be interpreted with caution.
