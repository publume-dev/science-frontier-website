To assess whether health risks associated with alcohol vary by beverage type and amount, researchers compared high, moderate, and low consumption against never or occasional drinking, examining deaths from any cause, cancer, and cardiovascular disease.

Compared with never or occasional drinkers, high alcohol consumption—more than 40 g/day for men and more than 20 g/day for women—was associated with a 24% higher risk of death from any cause, a 36% higher risk of cancer death, and a 14% higher risk of heart disease death. Moderate wine drinkers had a 21% lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease than never or occasional drinkers. In contrast, even low consumption of spirits, beer, or cider was associated with a 9% higher risk of cardiovascular death.

The findings are observational: drinking habits were self-reported at baseline, so the results cannot establish that beverage type caused the differences, and unmeasured lifestyle factors may explain part of the associations. In addition, the source provides no confidence intervals or a peer-reviewed manuscript, so the precision of the estimates cannot be assessed from this material.
