Large explosions generate low-frequency sound waves, or infrasound, that can travel great distances with little attenuation. In May 2026, a Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a static-fire test at Cape Canaveral. Researchers at Sandia National Laboratories used a network of 36 regional infrasound monitoring stations, some as far as 1,700 kilometers away, to analyze the event.

By analyzing the infrasound signals, the researchers independently confirmed the time and location of the explosion and estimated its yield at about 0.131 kilotons of TNT-equivalent. The detection range of up to 1,700 kilometers demonstrates that infrasound monitoring can effectively identify and characterize rocket accident explosions.

Depending on the fuel load, the explosion represented roughly 3-4% of the available chemical energy if the tanks were full, or about 6% if half full. The exact fuel quantity is not publicly known, so those percentages are based on plausible estimates under full-fill and half-fill scenarios. This finding provides new empirical data on cryogenic propellant explosions and highlights the value of infrasound for long-range explosion monitoring.
