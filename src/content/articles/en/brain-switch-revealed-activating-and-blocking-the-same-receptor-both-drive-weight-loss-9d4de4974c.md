A Cambridge-led mouse study, published in Nature Metabolism, aimed to understand the complex role of the GIP receptor in weight regulation. Previously, both activating and blocking this receptor promoted weight loss, but the mechanism was unclear.

The researchers used genetically engineered mice lacking GIPR in either the brainstem or hypothalamus, along with normal controls, and treated them with GIPR agonist, antagonist, and GLP-1 drugs to pinpoint where each treatment acts. They found that activating GIPR in the brainstem reduces appetite and leads to lower body weight, while blocking GIPR promotes weight loss through the hypothalamus, where GIPR acts as a brake limiting the brainstem's response to fullness signals.

Blocking GIPR was found to enhance the effects of emerging medicines that target the amylin receptor, suggesting GIPR antagonists may strengthen several classes of obesity treatments. This helps explain why drugs like MariTide, which combines GIPR antagonism with GLP-1 agonism and is in phase 3 clinical trials, can be effective. However, the study is based on mouse models; human applicability and specific clinical outcomes are not yet established.
