Researchers at the National University of Singapore report the first time a single strain of baker’s yeast has been engineered to respond to more than one color of light. They adapted a light-sensitive tool previously used in bacteria and mammalian cells into a red-light-responsive protein for yeast, named y-iLight, which works without added chemical cofactors beyond those naturally found in yeast.

Because blue light could also activate y-iLight, the researchers fused it to protein modules that suppress blue-light activation and tested combinations to find variants that kept red-light responsiveness while removing crosstalk, enabling independent control alongside the blue-light-responsive EL222 system. Using that two-color setup, they put two enzymes of luteolin production under separate red and blue light inputs and observed that changing the proportion and timing of light changed how the yeast made luteolin; they also observed the enzyme F3′H was less effective in later culture. Linking the flocculation gene FLO1 to a red-light switch let the yeast first make luteolin under blue light and then clump and sink under red light. The team also projected red and blue light through masks onto a thin layer of engineered yeast, producing patterned multicolored “living images” from different colored compounds.

The research was published in Nature Communications with DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-73399-0. The report describes the demonstrations qualitatively and gives no quantitative effect sizes, production yields, or explicit replication status, so the practical magnitude remains to be verified.
