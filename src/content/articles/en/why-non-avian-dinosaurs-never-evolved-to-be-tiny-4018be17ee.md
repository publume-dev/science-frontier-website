The smallest known non-avian dinosaurs weighed about one pound (roughly 450 grams) — more than 200 times as heavy as the 1.75-gram bee hummingbird, the smallest bird alive today. That gap is unlikely to be a quirk of preservation, the researchers note, because the same fossil sites also preserve much smaller animals such as mammals and lizards. In living faunas, about 75 percent of mammal species and 90 percent of bird species are smaller than the smallest known non-avian dinosaurs.

To test whether physiology set the limit, the researchers built mathematical models that incorporated physiological constraints. The models successfully reproduced body-size distributions in mammals, birds, and turtles, but they could not account for the relatively large minimum size of non-avian dinosaurs. The team suggests that competition with early small mammals may have kept dinosaurs from evolving into the smallest ecological niches. Birds, they point out, escaped the constraint through powered flight, which opened novel niches unavailable to terrestrial dinosaurs.

The researchers argue that the absence of truly tiny non-avian dinosaurs reflects a real ecological barrier rather than an incomplete fossil record. If their hypothesis holds, the dinosaur body-size floor was set not by basic physiology alone but by competition for the smallest niches — a barrier that flight allowed birds to bypass.
