A team led by University of California, Riverside geophysicists has developed an algorithm that uses GPS ground deformation data to identify locked fault segments (asperities) in subduction zones, where stress accumulates.[c1]

In a test on the Kamchatka subduction zone, the model highlighted a locked region where a magnitude-8.8 earthquake subsequently occurred in 2025.[c2] The research also found that the 2025 earthquake produced a much smaller tsunami than the 1952 Mw 9.0 earthquake in the same region, suggesting the shallowest part of the fault slipped less in the recent event.[c3]

The method cannot predict earthquake timing or tsunami size, but aims to identify regions of high seismic hazard for long-term planning.[c4] The team is now applying the approach to other subduction zones in Japan, Mexico, New Zealand, and the Pacific Northwest, and exploring its use on creeping faults like California's Hayward Fault.[c5]
