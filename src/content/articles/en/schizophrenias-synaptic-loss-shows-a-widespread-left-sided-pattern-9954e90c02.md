MRI-based studies have long linked schizophrenia to changes in brain volume, but their relation to synaptic-level alterations was unclear. The new study used PET imaging to directly measure synaptic density in the living brains of 122 people, including 29 with schizophrenia.

Compared with healthy participants, people with schizophrenia showed a pronounced and widespread reduction in synaptic connections across frontal, temporal, memory, and emotion-related regions, with considerably greater loss on the left side than the right. The pattern of synaptic loss did not match MRI-based changes in brain volume, suggesting separate biological processes. Brain regions with the greatest losses tended to have high concentrations of serotonin, GABA, and glutamate receptors. Computer simulations based on structural connectivity identified a region in the left frontal lobe as a likely starting point for the spread of synaptic loss.

This suggests that synaptic loss in schizophrenia may follow the brain's molecular and connectivity architecture, but this interpretation rests on correlational data and simulations rather than direct causal evidence.
