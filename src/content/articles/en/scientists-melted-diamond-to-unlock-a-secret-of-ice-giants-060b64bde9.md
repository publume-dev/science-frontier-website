Scientists had previously measured diamond's melting point in the lab, but experimental results differed from quantum-based model predictions by about 20%, a discrepancy that persisted for two decades. Diamond's behavior under extreme pressure is important not only for materials science but also for understanding the interiors of ice giants like Neptune and Uranus, where liquid carbon seas may exist.

Physicists at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory measured diamond's melting point under shock compression beyond 1 terapascal using X-ray diffraction at the Omega Laser Facility. The measurement was consistent with quantum-based theoretical models, resolving the 20% discrepancy seen in previous experiments. Under the studied conditions, solid diamond is less dense than liquid metallic carbon, meaning diamond can float on a liquid carbon sea—a condition that could support diamond rain inside ice giants like Neptune and Uranus.

The researchers' calculations suggest that a slower initial shock could melt the diamond capsule while using less power than current NIF experiments, potentially tripling the fusion energy output from the fuel. However, this claim comes from calculations rather than direct experimental demonstration, and the article notes that it is still far from engineering breakeven.
