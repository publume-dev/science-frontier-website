In tectonically active regions, large earthquakes often trigger landslides and erosion, but different landscapes respond differently. This study focuses on two lakes in the Southern Alps of New Zealand—Lake Mapourika and Lake Paringa—located near the Alpine Fault, which experienced a magnitude >8.0 earthquake in A.D. 1717. The researchers aimed to understand how topography controls post-seismic erosion processes.

Using lake sediments, the researchers combined carbon and nitrogen isotopes and molecular biomarkers to reconstruct erosion histories. They found that after the earthquake, the Paringa catchment shifted from eroding high-elevation surface soils to deeper soils over time, while the steeper Mapourika catchment maintained consistent erosion sources due to deep-seated landslides. This indicates that landscape steepness, hillslope-to-channel connectivity, and river transport capacity determine whether earthquakes fundamentally alter erosion processes.

The study highlights that topography plays a key role in determining how earthquakes modify erosion. Steep catchments tend to experience deep-seated landslides, while gentler catchments are dominated by shallow soil erosion. However, the report does not provide quantitative magnitudes for erosion depths or sediment volumes, describing trends qualitatively without specific data points.
