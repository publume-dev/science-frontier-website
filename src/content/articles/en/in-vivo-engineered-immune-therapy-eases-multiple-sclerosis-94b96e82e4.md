Autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis arise when the immune system attacks the body's own tissues. Conventional CAR-T cell therapies require modifying a patient's cells in the lab, which is complex and expensive.

In a small clinical trial, researchers used a lentivirus to deliver CAR genes directly to T cells inside the bodies of 16 people with multiple sclerosis or other autoimmune conditions. After treatment, participants generated more CAR T cells over time, which helped deplete B cells and reduce autoantibody levels. Newly generated B cells did not produce such autoantibodies, suggesting an immune reset. People with multiple sclerosis showed improved motor and cognitive function and reduced fatigue, while those with muscle conditions showed improved muscle strength and decreased inflammation.

This in vivo treatment is cheaper and faster to produce than conventional CAR-T therapies. However, the trial involved only 16 people and was monitored for about six months, so the investigators caution that the responses are not definitive evidence of efficacy or permanent restoration of immune tolerance.
