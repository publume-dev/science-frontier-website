The James Webb Space Telescope has revealed numerous “little red dots” in the early universe: they are compact and red, with stronger emission in ultraviolet and infrared than in visible light, along with weak X-ray emission. These features are typically interpreted as active galactic nuclei, but their true nature has remained debated.

A research team led by Pierluigi Rinaldi published a study on July 29 in The Astrophysical Journal proposing that many little red dots may not be a separate galaxy population but partly an observational bias. They used the lower-redshift spiral galaxy WISEA J123635.56+621424.2 (dubbed “Saguaro”) as a case study. The galaxy, at redshift 2, has a compact red nucleus matching little red dot characteristics, including stronger ultraviolet and infrared emission than visible, and weak X-ray emission detected by Chandra, indicating an obscured active galactic nucleus. When the researchers digitally shifted Saguaro to higher redshifts, its surrounding spiral structure became too faint to detect, leaving only the bright LRD-like center visible, supporting the idea that distant little red dots may appear isolated due to observational bias.

The authors caution that Saguaro cannot represent every little red dot; it illustrates one possible evolutionary stage. They emphasize that a broader census and future observations are needed to strengthen the interpretation. This case highlights the potential impact of observational bias on interpreting high-redshift objects.
