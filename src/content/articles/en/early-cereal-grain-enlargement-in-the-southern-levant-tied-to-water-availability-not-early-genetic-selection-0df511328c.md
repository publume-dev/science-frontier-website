At the Pre-Pottery Neolithic settlements of el-Hemmeh and Sharara in Wadi el-Hasa, Jordan, charred barley and emmer wheat were analyzed with three lines of evidence: morphology, weed ecology, and stable carbon isotopes. In the earliest phases, both cereals remained morphologically wild, with no evidence of domesticated nonshattering forms, yet grain size ranged from wild-sized to domesticated-sized.

Stable carbon isotope analysis showed that barley grains in the domesticated size range grew under wetter conditions, while smaller wild-sized grains grew under drier conditions, indicating that water availability influenced grain size. Weed ecological analysis linked the cereals to low-disturbance environments, challenging the assumption that larger grains had been selected through systematic tillage.

The authors conclude that developmental plasticity, rather than genetic change, accounted for the initial increase in cereal grain size in the Early Holocene southern Levant; genetic selection for larger grains may have begun later than previously thought. The report does not give exact sample sizes, stable isotope values, or statistical effect sizes, so those quantitative details would need to be checked against the underlying PNAS paper.
