Climate change is expected to push alpine vegetation toward species adapted to warmer conditions, a process known as thermophilization. However, whether the pace of this shift tracks local warming has been unclear due to a lack of large-scale, long-term data.

Researchers analyzed 724 long-term monitoring plots on 53 summits across all major European mountain ranges, spanning 21 years. The data confirm a significant overall shift toward warmth-associated species in alpine vegetation. Surprisingly, the rate of change at a given plot was only weakly explained by the locally measured warming. Instead, the presence of other warmth-associated species in the surrounding vegetation emerged as an important factor. At a broader spatial scale, mountain ranges that warmed more also showed a greater average shift toward warmth-associated species.

The findings challenge the simplistic assumption that local warming directly drives vegetation change. Instead, responses appear scale-dependent: at local scales, factors like the availability of warmth-associated species nearby matter more, while at regional scales, warming is more clearly linked to the shift.
