Petermann Glacier's floating ice tongue had shown signs of deformation and fractures before the event. Interferometric observations from April revealed deformation and cracks within the ice tongue.

Sentinel-1 radar images from August 3 showed deterioration along the center of the ice tongue, and the iceberg separated from the glacier's eastern side one day later. This is Petermann Glacier's largest loss of floating ice since 2012 and the biggest calving event recorded in the Arctic since 2020. The new iceberg is roughly the size of Manhattan and may be as much as 150 meters thick.

The calving has practical consequences for navigation and offshore activity, and Environment and Climate Change Canada is tracking the iceberg's path. Two additional ice islands, with estimated areas of about 97 and 87 square kilometers, could eventually detach as rifts continue spreading.
