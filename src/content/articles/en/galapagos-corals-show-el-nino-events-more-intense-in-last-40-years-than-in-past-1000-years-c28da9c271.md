El Niño is a major driver of global climate variability, yet its historical range in intensity over the past millennium is not fully understood. Researchers used oxygen isotope and strontium-to-calcium ratios in fossil and living coral skeletons from the Galápagos Islands to reconstruct sea surface temperatures over the past 1,000 years.

The coral records show that El Niño events over the past 40 years have been stronger on average than at any other time in the past 1,000 years. This increase exceeds what nearly all climate models would produce from natural causes alone, implying that human influence may have amplified El Niño intensity since the mid-20th century.

These findings suggest that El Niño intensification may be exceeding natural variability, which has important implications for understanding and predicting future climate extremes. However, the exact magnitude and statistical confidence of the observed intensification are not specified in the article, and not all climate models agree on the natural baseline, so these results warrant further investigation.
