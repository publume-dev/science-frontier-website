Mitochondrial proteins carry formyl groups, a chemical tag that is also found on bacterial proteins. According to the report, this shared tag lets mitochondrial proteins signal bacterial presence to the immune system, providing a potential route by which the body's own mitochondria could be mistaken for an infection.

In experiments described in the report, mice with acute lung inflammation showed elevated mitochondrial proteins in the bloodstream even though no live bacteria were present. The source of the proteins was inflamed endothelial cells, which released them through a process regulated by Pink1, a protein involved in clearing damaged mitochondria. When human neutrophils were exposed to the mitochondrial proteins, they became activated and generated high levels of oxidative stress without bacteria. Conversely, removing Pink1 from endothelial cells in mice reduced circulating mitochondrial proteins, lowered neutrophil accumulation in the lungs, and improved survival.

The results suggest that mitochondrial formyl tags can act as a bacteria-like inflammatory cue and that Pink1-dependent release may be a control point in sterile inflammation. The report did not, however, provide quantitative effect sizes or statistical significance for the survival and neutrophil-accumulation outcomes, and the evidence comes from mouse models and human cells in vitro, so clinical relevance for human inflammatory diseases is not yet established.
