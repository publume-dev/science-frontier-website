Peer review is the standard filter for grant applications, but it has known limits. In a 2018 study, 43 reviewers evaluating the same 25 grant applications barely agreed with one another. Because of this unreliability, the Swiss National Science Foundation in 2022 began using a lottery as a tie-breaker for proposals that reviewers could not confidently rank. The Research on Research Institute has logged more than a dozen funders that have used or tested partial lotteries, including 11 entries in its catalogue since 2022.

New Zealand’s Health Research Council has used a lottery for Explorer Grants since 2013. The British Academy started randomizing its Small Research Grants in 2022, and the trial has been extended to 2028. After the lottery was introduced, applications to the programme rose from 643 in 2022 to 1,257 in the 2025–26 round. The share of applications from Asian and Asian British researchers increased from 13% to 19%, and their share of awards from 11% to 17%.

Randomization does not remove judgment; it changes what a rejection means. Funders can tell applicants whether they fell short on quality or simply lost a draw among strong proposals, according to the article. The British Academy’s observed increases in applications and diversity happened alongside other factors, the article notes, so they cannot be attributed to the lottery alone. There is also no usable global data on the total amount of money distributed through randomized grant systems, making the scale of the shift hard to quantify.
