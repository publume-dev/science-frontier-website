The gut microbiome is the community of microorganisms living in the human digestive tract, and its composition is linked to health. While research has often focused on how antibiotics disrupt gut bacteria, whether other widely used drugs have similar long-term effects has lacked large-scale evidence in human populations.

An analysis of more than 2,500 participants from the Estonian Biobank showed that most medications examined were associated with differences in the gut microbiome, with many drugs having effects that persisted years after stopping. These long-lasting effects were not limited to antibiotics; antidepressants, beta-blockers, proton pump inhibitors, and benzodiazepines also exhibited distinctive microbial fingerprints. Benzodiazepines, commonly prescribed for anxiety, showed associations with the gut microbiome comparable to those seen with broad-spectrum antibiotics. Notably, medications within the same drug class did not necessarily affect the microbiome identically—for example, diazepam and alprazolam differed in their apparent disruption of gut microbes. In a smaller follow-up group, stool samples showed predictable shifts in gut microbes when participants started or stopped certain medications, supporting that the medications themselves may cause some of the observed differences.

Persistent effects were confirmed for proton pump inhibitors, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and antibiotics such as penicillins in combination and macrolides in the follow-up analysis. However, the follow-up involved a relatively small number of participants, which may limit the strength of the causal inference, and the original research paper was not directly cited, so specific effect sizes or statistical measures are unavailable.
