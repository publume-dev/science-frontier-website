In laser physics, the carrier-envelope phase (CEP) defines the exact position of peaks and troughs within each light pulse. It is a key parameter for achieving precise control over light-matter interactions. Previously, maintaining a stable CEP over long periods was a recognized challenge due to environmental disturbances and equipment drift.

The researchers built a laser system that generates 200,000 light pulses per second and characterized it in detail, showing that the CEP remains extremely stable across timescales from microseconds to hours, persisting for several hours. The study is published in Applied Physics B: Lasers and Optics, with detailed characterization performed by six Oldenburg researchers and collaborators from Lund University led by Nobel laureate Anne L'Huillier.

A stable CEP is considered crucial for controlled experiments on manipulating individual electrons, with potential applications for ultrafast transistors that operate at the speed of light. Although the system's CEP stability is described as "potentially record-breaking" by the lead author, the report does not provide specific quantitative values (e.g., milliradian levels) or a direct comparison against all previous systems.
