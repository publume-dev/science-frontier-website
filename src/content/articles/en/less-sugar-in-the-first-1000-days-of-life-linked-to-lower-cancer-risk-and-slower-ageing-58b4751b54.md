The study used the postwar sugar rationing in Britain as a natural experiment to examine how sugar exposure during the first 1,000 days of life affects long-term health. The report does not link to the primary research paper or provide detailed methodology, effect sizes with confidence intervals, or statistical significance levels.

People who had less sugar exposure during the first 1,000 days of life were less likely to develop breast, prostate, liver, rectal, and lung cancer later in life. The effect was strongest for liver cancer, with rates around 69% lower, and weakest for breast cancer, at 36% lower. People who experienced longer periods of sugar rationing during their first 1,000 days had longer telomeres, equivalent to about 2.2 years of slower biological ageing. Early-life sugar exposure was also associated with lower sugar consumption and healthier diets in adulthood, even 50 years later.

These findings suggest that early-life nutrition may have long-lasting effects on health, but the lack of detailed methodology and statistical information means the results should be interpreted with caution.
