Stainless steels typically rely on a chromium oxide surface layer for corrosion protection, but in chloride-containing environments this layer can break down, limiting their use in industrial applications such as water electrolysis.

Researchers at the University of Hong Kong have developed a new stainless steel, named SS-H2, which resists corrosion in chloride-containing environments at potentials up to 1700 mV, exceeding the water oxidation potential. The key is a 'sequential dual-passivation' mechanism, where a manganese-based protective layer forms on top of the conventional chromium oxide layer. The study was published in the journal *Materials Today*, and two patents for the technology have been authorized. In collaboration with a factory in Mainland China, tons of SS-H2-based wire have been produced, marking a significant step toward industrialization.

The researchers estimate that using SS-H2 could reduce the cost of structural materials in electrolyzers by about 40 times compared to materials like titanium coated with gold or platinum. However, this is an estimate based on material costs; actual system-level cost savings may vary with manufacturing and integration. Moreover, the long-term stability and performance of SS-H2 in commercial electrolyzers have not yet been demonstrated; only laboratory and pilot-scale production have been reported.
