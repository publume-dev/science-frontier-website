Urban trees are often assumed to improve air quality, but a study published in Science Advances reveals a potential downside. The research found that during May–July 2021 in Beijing, vegetation accounted for 52% of the chemicals that go on to form ozone, despite contributing only 10% of total volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions.

The researchers found that roughly 35% of trees in Beijing are isoprene emitters, including weeping willow and poplar trees. The chemical reactivity of these emissions increases with temperature: at 35 °C, the hydroxyl reactivity rate with VOCs from vegetation was seven times higher than at 20 °C, and vegetation's contribution to chemical reactivity rose from 21% at 20 °C to 74% at 35 °C.

The team also examined tree species in 24 megacities and predicted that isoprene emissions in cities like Sydney and Melbourne could be higher than in Beijing, with about 65% of trees in Sydney emitting isoprene. However, the extent to which these Beijing-specific findings apply to other cities depends on local tree composition and emission controls, which vary across the 24 megacities studied.
