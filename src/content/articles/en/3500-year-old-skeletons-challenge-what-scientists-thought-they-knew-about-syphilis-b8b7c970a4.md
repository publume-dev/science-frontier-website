Paleopathologists have long treated congenital infection signs in bones as evidence of venereal syphilis, but no confirmed biological or genetic evidence of pre-Columbian venereal syphilis has been found, and genetic studies of ancient remains from Europe and the Americas have revealed multiple treponemal diseases.

A study of 309 individuals from 16 archaeological sites across Vietnam, spanning 10,000 to 1,000 years before present, identified three children with dental and skeletal signs of congenital treponematosis. The epidemiological pattern at the Man Bac site, with most cases among children and adolescents, suggests a nonvenereal form such as yaws rather than venereal syphilis. The authors conclude that congenital transmission can occur in nonvenereal treponematoses, so congenital infection alone may not reliably distinguish venereal syphilis from other treponemal diseases in ancient remains.

Ancient DNA preservation in tropical regions like Southeast Asia is extremely poor, and destructive sampling raises ethical concerns, limiting genetic identification of ancient pathogens. Thus, the specific treponemal disease could not be genetically confirmed, and the interpretation relies on qualitative skeletal and epidemiological evidence, which carries inherent uncertainty.
