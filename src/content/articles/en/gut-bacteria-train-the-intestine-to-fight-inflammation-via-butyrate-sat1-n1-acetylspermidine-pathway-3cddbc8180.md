The gut microbiota is known to influence immune responses, and the metabolite butyrate has anti-inflammatory properties, but the underlying mechanism was unclear.

The study shows that butyrate activates the enzyme Sat1 in intestinal epithelial cells, leading to production of the metabolite N1-acetylspermidine, which induces IL-10-producing CD4+ T cells. In both mouse and human cell cultures, butyrate-treated epithelial cells secreted factors that induced IL-10-producing CD4+ T cells. In mice, oral butyrate treatment led to a sustained increase in IL-10 production by CD4+ T cells and protected against chemically induced colitis, with the effect persisting at least two weeks after butyrate withdrawal.

Butyrate or related metabolites could be a potential therapeutic strategy for inflammatory bowel disease, but the findings are based on mouse and cell culture studies; human relevance remains to be confirmed. Additionally, N1-acetylspermidine did not fully account for all immune-regulating activity, suggesting other factors contribute.
